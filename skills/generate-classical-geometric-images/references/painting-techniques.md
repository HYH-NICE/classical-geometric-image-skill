# Architectural Painting Techniques

Use this reference when the user supplies an architectural design draft, asks for a painted or illustrated result, or rejects a photorealistic background replacement. Preserve architecture first; the medium must explain the design rather than hide it.

## Routing contract

Classify the source artifact before choosing a medium: photograph, sketch, plan/elevation/section, axonometric, rendering, or mixed presentation board.

Route the painting technique from both architectural lineage and source artifact type; never choose a medium from cultural label alone.

A painted result must transform subject, architecture, ground, sky, light, shadow, edge language, pigment behavior, paper texture, and detail hierarchy into one coherent medium; a photorealistic subject pasted into a painted environment fails.

## Source-artifact routing

| Source artifact | Preserve | Preferred transformation | Do not do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Photograph | Camera height, horizon, lens character, topology, repeated counts, people and object invariants | Repaint the complete frame in one selected medium; translate reflections, skin, clothing, vegetation, sky, and architecture together | Keep a photographic person or object on a painted background |
| Freehand sketch | Stroke energy, silhouette, design intent, uncertain versus resolved lines | Clarify with ruled structural lines plus wash while retaining authored sketch variation | Replace ambiguity with invented finished construction |
| Plan/elevation/section | Orthographic projection, scale relationships, line hierarchy, annotations when requested | Ink or graphite construction with flat or graded wash; preserve projection | Turn it into perspective or erase measurement logic without permission |
| Axonometric | Parallel axes, cut planes, exploded relationships, module | Gouache, mineral-color, or paper-collage layers with consistent axonometric shadows | Introduce camera convergence or arbitrary perspective |
| Existing rendering | Massing, material zones, viewpoint, key light | Restyle the entire rendering through one line/pigment/substrate system | Merely add a texture overlay or filter |
| Mixed presentation board | Panel hierarchy, drawing types, registration, margins | Use one substrate, palette, title-free graphic hierarchy, and compatible media across panels | Collapse separate plan/elevation/section panels into one scene unless requested |

## Painting families

### Chinese architectural painting

Use **jiehua ruled-line architecture with ink and light color** for timber halls, towers, gates, axial compounds, and garden pavilions when construction and bay rhythm must stay precise. Establish ruled post, beam, eave, roof, stair, and paving lines first; add calligraphic vegetation and figures second; apply transparent ink wash or restrained color last. Keep voids breathable and let paper remain visible.

Use **ink-and-color garden rendering** for landscape-led gardens, rocks, water, moon gates, and pavilions. Keep the architecture ruled and exact while allowing rocks, trees, mist, and water to use softer brush edges. Do not dissolve structural joints or roof geometry into atmospheric wash.

Use **blue-and-green mineral color** only when the request or reference supports a deliberately archaic, courtly, or decorative landscape presentation. Treat it as a strong authored mode, not the default for every Chinese building.

### Islamic architectural painting

Use **precise ink with opaque watercolor** for courtyards, arcades, screens, domes, and architectural album views when the result should feel designed and materially rich. Define projection and geometry in ink; use controlled opaque color fields, limited gold only when requested, and crisp motif boundaries. Keep people, vegetation, water, and architecture in the same miniature-like edge and pigment language.

Use **pen-and-watercolor architectural study** for measured or documentary views. Keep perspective and masonry geometry crisp, then add transparent washes for stone, plaster, tile, water, and shadow. Avoid decorative manuscript borders unless explicitly requested.

Use **white technical ink on a dark ground** for geometry-first studies, vault logic, star grids, or muqarnas-derived construction. Use ruler-and-compass precision and explicit line hierarchy; do not present it as a photorealistic scene.

### Western classical architectural painting

Use **pen-and-wash architectural rendering** for classical, Renaissance, Baroque, Gothic, or civic stone architecture when proportion, light, and material need equal emphasis. Begin with graphite or ruled construction, reinforce selected edges with ink, then model stone and depth using transparent watercolor or gray/sepia wash.

Use **lavis or academy wash** for elevation, section, and competition-style sheets. Preserve line hierarchy; make shadows describe projection depth and daylight direction; keep paper margins and restrained tonal steps. Do not fake photographic depth of field.

Use **etching or engraved-line interpretation** for historical surveys, ruins, measured monuments, and archival plates. Build form through controlled hatching, cross-hatching, stipple, and line density. Use a limited ink palette on aged but legible paper; do not add random stains or illegible pseudo-text.

### Geometric and contemporary architectural presentation

Use **axonometric gouache** for modular, spatial, and geometric design studies. Keep parallel projection exact; separate planes with limited matte color; use one constructed shadow direction and consistent cut-plane hierarchy.

Use **architectural paper collage** for massing, material relationships, and editorial presentation only when the user asks for collage or the reference is already collage-led. Every subject must share the same cut-paper edge, grain, scale logic, and shadow convention. Avoid torn-paper zine styling unless explicitly requested.

Use **line-and-wash concept drawing** when the design is still exploratory. Keep decisive structural lines, lighter search lines, sparse wash, and visible paper. Do not over-resolve unresolved design decisions.

## Style strength

Choose one level and state it in the internal worksheet:

| Level | Use | Behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — restrained | Technical or approval-stage drawings | Geometry dominates; subtle paper, line, and wash; minimal expressive deviation |
| 2 — authored | Default architectural painting | Clear medium transformation with controlled brush, pigment, edge, and paper character |
| 3 — expressive | User explicitly requests a strong art treatment | Stronger abstraction and mark-making while hard-locked topology, projection, counts, and semantic minimum remain unchanged |

Default to level 2 for an explicit painting request. Default to level 1 for measured drawings unless the user asks otherwise.

## Unified medium checklist

- **Substrate:** Name paper, silk, dark ground, or collage stock and apply its grain across the complete frame.
- **Line system:** Define ruled, pen, brush, graphite, etched, or cut-paper edges plus a hierarchy of final, secondary, and guide lines.
- **Pigment system:** Define transparent wash, opaque watercolor, gouache, mineral color, ink, or limited mixed media.
- **Edge behavior:** Use one family of crisp, bleeding, dry-brush, hatched, or cut edges across architecture, figures, props, vegetation, and sky.
- **Shadow method:** Use wash, hatch, flat constructed shadow, or paper-layer shadow consistently; do not mix photographic shadows with painted forms.
- **Detail hierarchy:** Concentrate detail at the architectural and narrative focus; simplify secondary zones in the same medium.
- **Reflective objects:** Translate helmets, glass, water, metal, and glaze into the chosen pigment and edge system instead of preserving photographic specular detail.

## Cutout failure test

At thumbnail size, ask whether the person or object reads as a separate photographic layer. At full size, compare edge sharpness, noise/grain, highlight shape, color transitions, shadow construction, and detail frequency between subject and architecture. Any persistent mismatch fails rendering coherence even if contact shadows and perspective are correct.

## Reference anchors

- Chinese ruled-line architecture: Metropolitan Museum of Art, *Palace Banquet* and *Dragon Boat Regatta on Jinming Lake*.
- Chinese ink and color on paper: Metropolitan Museum of Art, Feng Zikai, *Ruled-painting*.
- Islamic architectural opaque watercolor: Metropolitan Museum of Art, *Interior of a Courtyard with Figures* and *Architectural Scene*.
- Islamic pen, ink, and watercolor architectural view: Metropolitan Museum of Art, Koodrutoollah, *A View of the Taj Mahal*.
- Pen-and-wash and architectural rendering terminology: Getty Art & Architecture Thesaurus entries for pen-and-wash drawings and renderings.
- Geometry-first white ink: British Museum, Timo Nasseri, *One and One #20*.

Use these as technique evidence, not as images to copy. Do not reproduce a living artist's distinctive style or claim historical purity from a broad family label.
